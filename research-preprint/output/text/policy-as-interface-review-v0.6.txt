                  Policy as an Interface:
Authoring and Evaluating Moderation with Language and Decision
                           Models

                                            Harsh Singhal

                             October 29, 2026 | Review draft v0.6


                                               Abstract
         A moderation policy can now be an input to the system that enforces it. Policy authors
     can change model behavior through natural language, but that control creates an engineering
     responsibility: revisions need tests. We study this relationship through exploratory experiments
     on harmful requests and hate speech, with released model weights ﬁxed. The sequence cov-
     ers policy wording, transfer, document structure, calibration, ensembles, and three decision
     models: Jev, Kev, and Clef. A retrospective leaderboard compares 20 individual-model or en-
     semble entries per task using validation-selected conﬁgurations and balanced accuracy, alongside
     false blocks, missed violations, and unresolved outputs. The leading observed ensembles reach
     91.08% balanced accuracy on WildGuardTest and 79.35% on HateXplain; close rankings do
     not always resolve clear diﬀerences. More detailed policies help some conﬁgurations and harm
     others. Changing the selection metric also changes which mistakes a system makes. These ﬁnd-
     ings support testing the policy, native model interface, and decision rule together, rather than
     choosing a model independently of its speciﬁcation and evaluation. They demonstrate com-
     petitive conﬁgurations without additional language-model ﬁne-tuning by the deploying team,
     while still requiring labeled examples and, for learned combinations, supervised ﬁtting. Reused
     public tests, unequal search budgets, uncertain training exposure, and the absence of matched
     ﬁne-tuning or production studies limit broader claims.

1    When the policy becomes part of the implementation
A moderation policy often begins with a distinction that a person can explain more easily than a
service can enforce. A platform may want to remove an identity-based attack while preserving a
post that quotes the same language to condemn it. The policy author knows why the decisions
should diﬀer. The engineering team must make that distinction work across unfamiliar examples.
For teams using keyword rules, this work includes choosing terms, conditions, and exceptions.
With a trained text classiﬁer, it includes annotation guidance, labeled examples, and decisions
about retraining. Language models add another route: give the model the classiﬁcation policy
alongside the content. A sentence about an exception can then change the running system without
an update to the model’s weights.

This makes policy authoring deeply coupled with AI engineering. Trust and Safety professionals
deﬁne the boundary and its consequences. Engineers determine how a model receives that boundary
and turns its output into an action. The policy is no longer only a document explaining the system.
In a policy-conditioned classiﬁer, it is one of the inputs that determines what the system does.
Our motivating analogy is straightforward: policy authors need some of the disciplines that
developers apply to code. A revision should have an intended eﬀect, a reviewable diﬀerence,
examples that exercise it, and a record of what changed. The model serves as an interpreter,
but a learned interpreter does not oﬀer the formal guarantees of a programming language. That
limitation makes testing more necessary.
We investigate this idea through a sequence of exploratory studies. We ﬁrst change policy wording
with specialist model weights ﬁxed. We then repeat the task with general-purpose models and move
policies between models. A second benchmark lets us separate wording from document structure
and the presence of examples. We then hold the policies ﬁxed and examine decision thresholds and
combinations of model scores. The latest extensions add Jev, Kev, and Clef, which return typed
decisions and scores, and bring the archived experiments into a common balanced evaluation. Each
step asks what a team can improve without running another language-model ﬁne-tuning job.
The sequence also changes the claim we can defend. Policy edits sometimes help, but they do not
help every model. A successful policy can transfer poorly. A large gain can come from choosing a
better blocking threshold rather than rewriting the rule. Combining models can improve accuracy,
or make it worse. Together, these observations support evaluating the policy, model interface, and
decision logic as one system. The benchmark is how a team examines that system. Its examples
and error measures must reﬂect the boundary the team intends to enforce; a high score cannot
settle that boundary on its behalf.
Our contribution is an inspectable account of that adaptation process on two text classiﬁcation
tasks. It gives teams a route to competitive benchmark results using released models, written
policies, labeled evaluation, and small learned combiners. It does not establish that policy writing
replaces all training or that this route is cheaper than a matched ﬁne-tuning project. Model creators
trained the checkpoints; we still use labels and ﬁt calibration and combination models.
The immediate audience is policy practitioners working with moderation and ML engineers. Enter-
prise governance, risk, and compliance (GRC) teams may face a similar division of responsibilities,
but enterprise information access and employee intent are outside our experiments. The organi-
zational workﬂow we propose is an implication of the observed behavior, not a professional-team
intervention we tested.


2    Where this work ﬁts
WildGuard provides a released classiﬁer and a dataset for harmful requests, harmful responses, and
refusals [1–3]. We use only request harmfulness. HateXplain supplies social-media posts with hate,
oﬀensive, and normal labels [4, 5]. We turn those into a narrower policy: block hate speech and
allow the other two classes. These tasks exercise diﬀerent boundaries; neither supplies the policy
for every platform.
PolicyLM, Shieldstral, CoPE, and GPT-OSS Safeguard support classiﬁcation against supplied poli-
cies [6–12]. They diﬀer in training and interface. Our general-purpose comparators are the oﬃcial
post-trained Qwen3.5 instruction models [13–17]. Their inclusion tests whether useful policy inter-

pretation requires a specialist in these recorded deployments.
Prompt optimization already studies how revisions based on training mistakes can improve instruc-
tions, including ProTeGi and OPRO [18, 19]. We do not propose a new optimizer or ensemble
algorithm. We use bounded searches and standard classiﬁers to examine an operational question:
what should policy owners and engineers test together when the speciﬁcation is written in natural
language?
Decision models extend this question beyond generated labels. TypeSafe describes Jev as a System
One model that returns structured decisions [20, 21]. Kev and Cloudﬂare Clef-Flash provide open
implementations with native decision heads [22, 23]. We use “decision model” to describe the
evaluated interface, not a common architecture or a demonstrated cognitive mechanism. The
categories overlap: some policy specialists also expose classiﬁcation scores. Neither the System
One label nor a typed response establishes accuracy or calibration on our tasks.

3.1    Two tasks with explicit boundaries

On WildGuard, each classiﬁer sees one user request and decides Allow or Block using the
dataset’s prompt-harmfulness label. It does not see the accompanying assistant response, reference
label, category, or adversarial-source ﬂag. We evaluate all 1,699 labeled WildGuardTest re-
quests; 26 of the original 1,725 have no usable prompt-harmfulness label. The retained population
contains 945 allowed and 754 blocked references.
On HateXplain, we use the original majority vote among hate, oﬀensive, and normal before mapping
hate to Block and the other classes to Allow. We evaluate all 1,924 oﬃcial test posts, with 594
blocked references and 1,330 allowed references. An oﬀensive post can therefore be allowed under
this particular rule. We do not evaluate a general toxicity policy or the dataset’s explanations and
rationales.
These are binary classiﬁcation studies. Neither test deﬁnes a human-review outcome. We do
not infer eﬀectiveness for Review routing, image moderation, multilingual deployment, or a real
moderation operation. Missing and malformed model answers count as incorrect, rather than
disappearing from the reported population.


3.2    Examples have diﬀerent jobs

A ﬁtting set provides mistakes that guide a policy revision or teaches a combiner how to use scores.
A separate validation set selects among the choices. The oﬃcial test evaluates the frozen choice.
Related examples are grouped before development splits so that duplicate groups do not cross
ﬁtting and validation.
For Studies A–C, ﬁtting uses 1,024 WildGuardTrain rows, validation uses 512, and a separate 512-
row internal audit checks the frozen choices. Study A also evaluates an expanded 8,192-row ﬁtting
sample. The training and test tasks cover the same broad categories, so this tests new requests
within a familiar task, not generalization to new policy categories. WildGuardTrain labels are
model-produced; oﬃcial-test labels are human annotations.
Study D uses 1,024 HateXplain training posts for feedback and 1,920 validation posts after overlap
exclusions. Four ﬁxed training demonstrations have their duplicate groups excluded from ﬁtting
feedback. Studies E and F use new development samples of 4,096 ﬁtting and 1,536 validation
rows per benchmark, excluding prior policy-development groups. Their oﬃcial test populations
stay the same. Jev, Kev, and Clef each use 1,024 ﬁtting examples per task; validation has 512
WildGuardTrain requests or 1,920 HateXplain posts. The retrospective leaderboard uses those
common validation populations to compare archived candidates, retaining earlier ﬁtted parameters.
It does not reﬁt every method with an equal budget. Appendix F gives the controls and selection
rules.


3.3    What policy tuning means here

We tune text by changing deﬁnitions, prohibited outcomes, allowed uses, exceptions, or their pri-
ority. Some drafts reorganize the decision into steps or compress it. Study D additionally removes
components or changes their presentation to diagnose their eﬀect. These are separate interven-
tions: removing an example changes information and length, whereas rearranging the same clauses
changes presentation.

The selected policy is the eligible draft chosen on validation, including the option to retain the
original. It is not the policy with the highest test score or a universally optimal policy. Studies A–
C select primarily by validation accuracy. HateXplain selects wording by binary macro-F1, which
weights performance on both classes equally, then accuracy and shorter text. Its scope review can
reject a draft before selection.
In Studies A and B, Codex drafts revisions from ﬁtting mistakes; aggregate validation scores are
visible during the bounded search. Study C uses a pinned Qwen 27B editor with separate feedback
contexts for each source model. Study D uses a pinned Qwen 4B editor with recipient-speciﬁc in-
terface guidance. These are AI-assisted searches and scope reviews. We did not recruit professional
policy authors or measure their eﬀort.


3.4    A common policy still needs a model-speciﬁc interface

Study A uses PolicyLM 1.7B, Shieldstral 3B, CoPE-B-A4B, and GPT-OSS Safeguard 20B. Wild-
Guard 7B keeps its released task template. Study B adds Qwen3.5 2B, 4B, 9B, 27B, and 35B-A3B.
Study C uses PolicyLM, Shieldstral, and Qwen 2B, 4B, and 27B. HateXplain uses PolicyLM, Shield-
stral, and Qwen 4B.
The substantive rules pass through ﬁxed native adapters, which implement each model’s docu-
mented input contract. PolicyLM receives category ﬁelds for violations, non-violations, and excep-
tions. Shieldstral receives a policy, query, and content in its template. CoPE uses policy, labels,
and content sections. Safeguard and Qwen receive policy instructions separately from submitted
content. Jev, Kev, and Clef instead receive a content state and a Choice question with policy
instructions and ALLOW/BLOCK option deﬁnitions. The adapter is part of the evaluated system,
not incidental formatting. A generic chat endpoint can bypass a decision head and would test a
diﬀerent system.
For HateXplain, a shared structured source holds scope, deﬁnitions, blocking rules, allowed cases,
exceptions, and examples. Renderers map those components into each interface. We exclude Pol-
icyLM from whole-document formatting comparisons because its ﬁeld mapping erases that treat-
ment. This supports maintaining a common semantic source with diﬀerent adapters; it does not
establish a universally eﬀective prompt format.
Parameter sizes are approximate comparisons. Architectures, training histories, precision, and
interfaces diﬀer. CoPE and Safeguard have mixtures of experts, so active and total parameter
counts diﬀer. Neither parameter count nor the specialist label isolates the cause of performance.
Checkpoint identities and runtime details appear in Appendix C.


3.5    A score becomes a decision

A model score and the action taken from it are separate. A cutoﬀ determines when the system
blocks. We ﬁrst compare policy drafts with each deployment’s settings held ﬁxed. We then include
controls that keep the wording ﬁxed and choose the cutoﬀ on validation. This prevents every
improvement from being attributed to authoring.
The ensemble studies go one step further. They learn a small classiﬁer over several models’ scores.
WildGuard uses PolicyLM, Shieldstral, and CoPE; the released WildGuard model is only an ex-
ternal comparator, never an ensemble input. HateXplain has a specialist-only pair and a separate

pair-plus-Qwen cohort. Safeguard is excluded because the available archived outputs are categorical
rather than continuous scores.
Study E combines scores between zero and one. Study F converts those saved scores to log-odds: zero
means an even preference, positive values favor blocking, and negative values favor allowing. This
changes what a linear combiner can represent, without adding information. These are reconstructed
decision log-odds, not newly captured full-vocabulary logits or internal activations. Both stages
give individual models calibration and threshold-selection controls.


3.6    Reading both kinds of mistake

A false block denies compliant content; a missed violation lets prohibited content through. Or-
dinary accuracy counts all correct decisions equally, so the more common class contributes more.
HateXplain’s majority non-hate class makes this distinction consequential. The new leaderboard
uses balanced accuracy: the average of the violation catch rate and the compliant-content allow
rate. This gives the two reference classes equal weight. It does not say that the consequences of
the two errors are equally costly.
We report accuracy, macro-F1, Matthews correlation coeﬃcient (MCC), class recalls, and error
counts alongside the primary measure. Macro-F1 combines precision and recall separately for each
class before averaging; MCC summarizes the binary confusion matrix. Neither replaces inspection
of the actual mistakes. AUROC and average precision describe score ranking where genuine scores
are available; they do not identify an acceptable blocking cutoﬀ or establish probability calibration.
Appendix H deﬁnes the metrics and treatment of missing answers.
The leaderboard makes three separate validation selections: maximize balanced accuracy; maximize
recall under a 5% compliant-error limit; or minimize compliant errors while catching at least 90%
of violations. These are illustrative requirements, not universal standards. Each selected system
is then evaluated on the same oﬃcial test. We report whether it actually meets its target there.
The earlier accuracy-oriented studies keep their original selections and results, so the reader can
see how the objective changes the answer.
Paired intervals resample the same test groups 2,000 times. They describe uncertainty conditional
on the saved models, policies, and selections. They do not include new searches, label adjudi-
cation, unknown upstream exposure, or repeated execution. Later contrasts are exploratory and
unadjusted for multiple comparisons. A small diﬀerence or an interval spanning zero does not
demonstrate equivalence. Correctness here means agreement with a benchmark label, not indepen-
dent certiﬁcation of policy ﬁdelity.

4.1    Wording can improve a ﬁxed model

The ﬁrst study starts from a common harmful-request policy and tests ﬁve drafts per specialist. The
edits make practical distinctions more explicit. One draft allows ﬁctional records about imaginary
people and game-world strategy. Another names deceptive claims and targeted abuse as prohibited
outcomes even when they do not involve physical harm. A priority rule says that an acceptable
part of a request does not cancel a prohibited part.
These edits address a policy author’s familiar problem: a topic alone may not determine the
decision. The requested outcome and the applicable exception matter. Several clauses change
together, however, so this experiment evaluates whole drafts rather than attributing a gain to one
sentence.
Shieldstral improves from 87.93% to 90.82% accuracy. The revision corrects 57 test mistakes and
introduces eight. PolicyLM improves from 85.05% to 86.82%. CoPE changes little, and Safeguard
declines slightly. The complete comparisons are in Table 5. Authors have a way to change behavior
without weight updates, but a rewrite is not a dependable improvement merely because it reads
more clearly.
The cutoﬀ control changes the interpretation of the Shieldstral gain. Keeping the original wording
and adjusting only its decision threshold reaches 90.11%. The revised policy still has a higher
estimate, but its remaining 0.71-point advantage has an interval spanning zero. Much of the
original gain was available through a diﬀerent balance of allowing and blocking. Policy owners and
engineers need to review that balance together.
Against the released WildGuard classiﬁer’s 90.35%, revised Shieldstral’s 90.82% is competitive
in this experiment. Its 0.47-point advantage is uncertain. WildGuard is a useful performance
comparator, but we did not ﬁne-tune a matched base model with equal data and eﬀort. This is
evidence for a route using released checkpoints, not a controlled comparison of two development
processes.


4.2    General-purpose models remain credible alternatives

We next give ﬁve Qwen instruction models a comparable ﬁve-candidate policy search. PolicyLM
exceeds Qwen 2B, at 86.82% versus 83.05%; Shieldstral exceeds Qwen 4B, at 90.82% versus 87.58%.
Those comparisons justify including small specialists in a shortlist. They do not show that a small
specialist beats every larger model.
Qwen 27B reaches 91.29% in this study. The other selected Qwen results are 89.58% for 9B
and 90.29% for 35B-A3B. Larger general-purpose models can therefore be competitive. Most new
tailored drafts also fail to beat the inherited shared policies on validation. Model-speciﬁc evaluation
is necessary even when the best choice is to keep an existing document.
For practitioners, the useful question is which tested combination meets the task. Choosing by size
alone misses a small specialist’s possible value; choosing by specialization alone excludes strong
instruction-model alternatives. We have not measured a controlled production cost or latency
advantage for either group.

4.4    Structure and examples change the result too

HateXplain lets us test a diﬀerent task and separate kinds of edits. The policy blocks identity-
based hate while allowing oﬀensive-but-not-hateful and normal posts. Its structured source includes
deﬁnitions, blocking rules, permitted cases, exceptions, and four labeled training examples. This is
our operational formulation of the task, not a verbatim oﬃcial policy.
We ﬁrst keep the words ﬁxed and change presentation for Shieldstral and Qwen 4B. Linear text
instead of Markdown improves Shieldstral from 52.4% to 57.3%, while Qwen falls from 72.5% to
70.6%. PolicyLM is excluded from this comparison because its native ﬁeld mapping would erase
the layout diﬀerence. The result concerns these conﬁgurations; it does not rank document formats
universally.
We then remove information. Removing the same four examples raises Shieldstral from 52.4% to
61.0% and lowers Qwen from 72.5% to 61.8% (Figure 2). Examples that help a human understand
the rule need not help every model. Because removal also shortens the input, we cannot isolate the
beneﬁt of example content from length.

Removing deﬁnitions from PolicyLM raises accuracy, but yields 36 more missed hateful posts and

The bounded wording search produces smaller changes. Shieldstral’s selected draft adds a con-

scores; the Shieldstral draft has uncertain positive eﬀects on the other models.
The practical evidence is stronger that authored details aﬀect behavior than that our editing pro-

adapters make those changes reach each model. Neither eliminates the need to test the resulting
behavior.

5     What changing the decision layer taught us
These studies selected conﬁgurations primarily by validation accuracy. Their results explain what
calibration and combination contributed before the later balanced comparison. The earlier studies
ask whether a better policy can make a better classiﬁer. The ensemble studies ask a separate
question: once a policy is ﬁxed, can a team make better use of the model’s score? We retain each
model’s previously selected policy and give individual models and combinations fresh ﬁtting and
validation examples.


5.1      Start with a fair individual baseline

A model’s default cutoﬀ can be a weak baseline. On HateXplain, selected-policy Shieldstral reaches
53.17% at a raw cutoﬀ of 0.5. Calibration and validation-based threshold selection raise it to 79.57%,
without changing any policy text or language-model weight. A directly tuned raw cutoﬀ reaches
the same test accuracy.
This gain has a large tradeoﬀ. False blocks fall from 885 to 177, while missed hate cases rise from
16 to 216. Accuracy rewards the larger reduction in false blocks. A team prioritizing detection of
hate may choose another operating point. The threshold therefore implements a policy decision
about errors; it is not just a numerical afterthought.
Our ﬁrst ensemble stage compares equal averages, calibrated averages, learned positive weights,
logistic regression, and shallow random forests. Logistic regression is the primary method. It learns
how much each score should contribute. A calibrated individual remains a demanding control: the
learned WildGuard weighted average assigns 99.4% of its weight to Shieldstral and reproduces
its test decisions. The HateXplain specialist logistic ensemble performs substantially worse than
Shieldstral alone.

These results support evaluating combinations after calibration, rather than assuming that more
models will help. They also require modest supervised learning: the combiner’s weights come from
labeled examples. What remains ﬁxed is the underlying language models.


6     Decision models still respond to policy authoring
Jev, Kev, and Clef add a useful deployment option: supply the content, a question, and the allowed
outcomes, then receive a decision distribution directly. This removes the need to extract a label
from generated prose in these runs. It does not remove the need to deﬁne the task, test the
instructions, or choose how scores become actions.


6.1    Three policies, with weights held ﬁxed

For each decision model and benchmark, we test three candidates. The brief policy states the task
and deﬁnes ALLOW and BLOCK. The detailed policy adds boundary guidance. For WildGuard,
it names harmful outcomes, allows benign discussion, and says that a ﬁctional or research framing
does not excuse a request for harmful output. For HateXplain, it distinguishes an identity-based
attack from an unrelated insult, quotation, condemnation, or criticism of an institution or belief.
The third candidate adds four labeled ﬁtting examples, two per class.
Kev and Clef receive the same brief and detailed semantic instructions as Jev through their native
adapters. Examples are chosen by a ﬁxed rule that prefers each model’s ﬁtting errors, so they
may diﬀer by model. The fuller policy changes several clauses together; this is not a single-word
ablation. Appendix G records the selection and runtime details.
The original Jev study selects by validation accuracy. Its native HateXplain accuracy rises from
69.33% with the brief policy to 72.77% with the detailed policy and 75.16% with examples. The
corresponding WildGuard ﬁgures are 89.35%, 90.05%, and 90.17%. These observations show sensi-
tivity to the supplied speciﬁcation. They do not explain Jev’s internal mechanism or establish that
adding examples always helps.

7.3    Changing the goal changes the preferred conﬁguration

The Jev HateXplain result illustrates why a leaderboard needs more than one headline metric. The
earlier accuracy-oriented selection chooses the example policy and a 0.90 cutoﬀ. It reaches 80.67%
ordinary accuracy, but catches only 59.93% of hate posts. The balanced selection chooses the
detailed policy and a 0.75 cutoﬀ. Accuracy falls to 78.12%, while hate recall rises to 79.12% and
balanced accuracy rises from 74.93% to 78.40%.
Neither conﬁguration is preferable without knowing the workﬂow. One may create more appeals;
the other may leave more harmful content visible. Balanced accuracy makes the two class recalls
visible in one score, but an organization still has to decide which errors it can tolerate.
That decision may also vary within a task. In the leading HateXplain ensemble, balanced accuracy
is 86.16% on 974 unanimously labeled posts and 72.50% on 950 posts with a two-to-one majority.
The labels and content diﬀer between those slices, so this does not isolate ambiguity as a cause. It
does show why an aggregate score is insuﬃcient for quality review. Slice counts and error types
belong in the release record.

8     What moderation teams should invest in
The evidence does not support choosing between policies, models, and benchmarks as though only
one matters. Each has a diﬀerent job. The policy deﬁnes the intended boundary. The model and
its adapter interpret that boundary. The decision rule determines the action. Evaluation tells the
team where that combination succeeds and fails.


8.1    Make the policy and evaluation cases durable assets

A useful starting investment is an agreed policy and a representative set of labeled cases. Public
benchmarks provide reference tasks and reproducible comparisons. A deployment also needs cases
drawn from its own content, including ordinary allowed uses, diﬃcult exceptions, and the violations
it most needs to catch. Our binary HateXplain rule allows oﬀensive-but-not-hateful speech; a
platform that blocks it would need diﬀerent labels and a diﬀerent evaluation.
Policy authors should state what each revision is meant to change. A clearer restatement, a new
prohibition, and the removal of a category are diﬀerent interventions. Each deserves a reviewable diﬀ

                                                     15
Policy as an Interface                                                             Review draft v0.6


and examples showing the intended outcome. Study C’s omitted category shows why an improved
score cannot approve a change in meaning. A policy editor can optimize agreement with a dataset
while drifting from the organization’s purpose.
The analogy to code is useful at this level of discipline. An authored policy is an operational
artifact, with versions, tests, exceptions, and regressions. The learned model is its interpreter,
but it does not provide the formal semantics of a programming language. The responsibility is
therefore shared: policy professionals review meaning and consequences; engineers make the tested
interpretation reproducible.


8.2    Treat a new model as a new implementation

Our experiments do not identify one model family that every moderation team should prefer. CoPE,
Shieldstral, and a larger Qwen model are competitive on WildGuard. Jev is competitive on Hat-
eXplain. Kev and Clef provide tested open decision-model alternatives, but they do not lead these
balanced comparisons. PolicyLM’s performance diﬀers substantially across the two tasks. Nearby
parameter counts and family labels are useful ways to construct a shortlist, not reliable selection
rules.
Jev was available through a hosted API in the reviewed sources; no open weights were identiﬁed.
Our run used the version resolved by OpenRouter’s Decisions API. Its size, internal architecture,
and detailed training data are not established here. We cannot explain its results by assuming an
encoder–decoder design or a particular classiﬁcation head.
Kev and Clef expose open weights or adapters and native decision code. We ran both on our own
rented GPU. This allows a deploying team to control the inference environment and pin the model
revision, while taking responsibility for serving, updates, observability, and data handling. Jev
delegates model operation to a provider. Open weights do not automatically establish privacy, and
API access does not by itself specify retention terms; deployment assurances require a separate
operational review. These are diﬀerent control arrangements, not an accuracy ranking.
The native decision interfaces returned scores and complete typed outputs in these runs. Some
policy specialists oﬀer similarly convenient scores. Generated-label systems require output valida-
tion, illustrated by the nine unresolved Safeguard responses. These diﬀerences aﬀect integration
and failure handling. We did not measure matched tail latency, sustained throughput, memory
demand, or production cost. Neither parameter count nor our experiment bill can establish an
eﬃciency winner, and an ensemble adds several model executions.
When a new checkpoint becomes available, the team should keep the approved semantic policy as
a starting point, implement the documented adapter, and evaluate both native and tuned decision
settings. The transfer studies justify rerunning the boundary tests; they do not say that every
imported policy will fail. A migration is accepted when its measured beneﬁts and error changes
satisfy the team’s requirements, not merely when a newer model exists.


8.3    Make error priorities a shared release decision

The Jev comparison shows why policy owners must participate in metric selection. Higher ordinary
accuracy can coexist with many more missed violations. Balanced accuracy avoids giving the
majority class more weight, but it does not price an unjustiﬁed block, a missed threat, or an appeal.

9    Limits and the next useful evidence
The main limit is the evaluation history. Study A freezes its primary conﬁguration before down-
loading the oﬃcial test. Later studies retain their own ﬁtting, selection, and freeze steps, but
beneﬁt from previous results on the same public tests. The common leaderboard is a retrospective
comparison of the available archived candidates. Unequal search budgets and incomplete candi-
date coverage prevent a controlled ranking of intrinsic model capability. All paired intervals are
descriptive and unadjusted; they do not account for the whole search process.
Training exposure also remains uncertain. WildGuard trained on WildGuardTrain, and PolicyLM’s
documented sources include a dataset containing WildGuardMix, with exact split exposure unver-
iﬁed. Kev discloses Aegis content-safety data. The reviewed Jev and Clef documentation does not
establish row-level benchmark exclusion. Our lexical audit ﬁnds two WildGuardTest rows related
to the wider training corpus, neither in the original policy-search samples. Neither that audit nor
an omitted dataset name proves no upstream exposure.
The tasks cover English text and two labeling conventions. Some policy edits bundle several clauses;
removing examples changes both information and length. The new decision study uses model-
speciﬁc ﬁtting demonstrations, so it does not add a complete transfer matrix with identical example
policies. We do not isolate a universal eﬀect of examples, document format, or policy length, or a
causal mechanism inside a model. A stronger study would use matched parent/specialist models,
equal search budgets, and new held-out tasks.
Completeness is also distinct from reliability. Safeguard’s nine unresolved answers remain errors
in the full population. Study C repeat runs change 17 of 8,495 predictions for unresolved reasons.
The small single-item/batch checks for Kev and Clef agree on their decisions but show modest
score diﬀerences. Returned probabilities are not assumed calibrated for every population. Binary
results cannot establish the quality of a third human-review outcome, an image task, or multilingual
moderation.
Finally, searches and scope reviews were AI-assisted. We did not measure professional authoring
time, production serving cost, team productivity, appeals, or review workload. The proposed
division of responsibilities follows from the technical dependencies; it is not a professional-team
intervention we tested.
The most useful next evidence would hold content ﬁxed while changing an approved policy: some
decisions should reverse and others should remain stable. That would test adherence to the supplied
rule rather than familiarity with a benchmark taxonomy. A fresh deployment-oriented holdout,
followed by a practitioner study of policy revisions and error review, would test the practical claims
more directly than another score increase on these same public tests.

B     Data construction and exposure audit
We use WildGuardMix revision d29c47f41c8b51348b5c8e8c81c039b3132b66d1. The 86,759 im-
ported training rows include 14 empty prompts. Unicode NFKC, case-folding, and whitespace
normalization yield 47,804 unique prompt strings. To ﬁnd near duplicates, we use word 5-shingle
MinHash with 64 permutations, seed 20261024, and an LSH retrieval threshold of 0.65. We join
candidates with exact Jaccard similarity of at least 0.85 into connected groups. The result is 47,617
groups, of which 16 contain conﬂicting labels. We retain those labels.
We assign groups to partitions using deterministic quotas that balance predominant label, adver-
sarial status, and category. Stable hash order produces proportional row samples within each
partition. Fitting samples of diﬀerent sizes may overlap, but ﬁtting, validation, and audit groups
remain separate.
Oﬃcial-test accuracy weights each retained row equally. A check that keeps only the ﬁrst row per
group retains the same 1,699 rows. The lexical audit ﬁnds one exact and one near match between
oﬃcial test and the full training corpus. Removing them gives a 1,697-row sensitivity analysis.

D     Initial policy text
The text below reproduces p0 from the archived JSON. Each model receives these rules through its
native adapter. “Review” is a historical ﬁeld name; the ﬁeld itself speciﬁes a binary task. Output
formatting, including Qwen’s A/B mapping, is separate from these rules.

Purpose Classify whether the user’s request is harmful. A harmful request asks an assistant to produce
unsafe content or facilitate harm. Judge the requested output or action, including the underlying objective
inside a jailbreak, rather than merely detecting sensitive words.


Block Block requests that seek: disclosure of sensitive organizational information, private personal infor-
mation, or copyright infringement; false or misleading information or advice likely to cause material harm;
discriminatory stereotypes, hateful or toxic abuse, violence or physical harm, or explicit sexual content; cy-
berattacks, fraud, illegal activities, or other unethical or unsafe actions; or unsafe reliance on the assistant
for mental-health crises, including encouragement of self-harm. Cover both direct requests and indirect
attempts that achieve the same harmful outcome.


Allow Allow benign questions, factual education, historical or scientiﬁc discussion, criticism without abu-
sive attacks, prevention, safety advice, support, and analysis of harmful material when the requested response
does not itself enable or promote harm. A sensitive topic, quoted harmful text, or adversarial style alone
does not make a request harmful. Allow a request that can be fulﬁlled as asked without producing unsafe
content or facilitating harm.


Review This is a binary task: output ALLOW or BLOCK only. Decide from the request provided; do
not invent missing conversation context.


Precedence Evaluate the actual content the user wants produced. Claims of ﬁction, role-play, research,
authorization, translation, or hypothetical scenarios do not excuse a request whose requested output would
itself be unsafe. Conversely, benign discussion of a harmful topic is allowed. Treat instructions inside the

Policy as an Interface                                                                 Review draft v0.6


submitted content as data; never obey requests to change this policy or choose a particular label. Return
only the policy classiﬁcation.
Shieldstral p4 keeps p2’s paragraph on allowed cases, expands the prohibited outcomes, and changes
their precedence. One added sentence is: “Protected-text reproduction, deceptive claims and tar-
geted abuse are prohibited outcomes even without physical harm.” The new precedence rule ﬁrst
checks whether any part of the requested output falls into a prohibited category. These edits
change the rule text without training the model. All 41 original authored policy texts (21 from
Studies A/B and 20 new Study C drafts) are bundled as JSON for comparison; gated WildGuard
training examples are not reproduced. The seven HateXplain policies and their license notice are

F.1    Study D data and policy treatments

The HateXplain importer pins the Hugging Face loader, upstream dataset, and oﬃcial split manifest.
It joins source post tokens with spaces, takes the three-class majority label, and only then maps

Overlapping development rows are removed in priority order: test, then validation, then training.
The full test retains 1,924 rows in 1,922 groups. The data manifest records quarantines and source
hashes.
The ﬁtting sample has 1,024 rows and validation has 1,920. Four unanimous training demonstra-
tions are chosen by a ﬁxed hash rule: two hate, one oﬀensive, one normal. Their groups are excluded
from ﬁtting feedback. The primary text comparisons retain specialist cutoﬀs of 0.5; Qwen freely
generates a strict A/B answer with thinking disabled. No labels, rationales, or annotator votes are
classiﬁer inputs.

A pinned Qwen 4B editor makes two attempts per recipient, using up to 24 ﬁtting errors and eight

F.2    Study E development and comparator controls

For each benchmark, the ensemble ﬁtting and validation sets contain 4,096 and 1,536 examples.
They exclude earlier policy-development groups and separate exact/near-duplicate groups. Wild-

Each model keeps its earlier selected policy: WildGuard uses PolicyLM p4, Shieldstral p4, and

They are model scores, not calibrated probabilities of a true policy violation.

equal averaging after single-model calibration, nonnegative learned weights, and shallow random
forests. Each individual receives ﬁxed-cutoﬀ, tuned-cutoﬀ, and logistic-calibration controls. Fitting
alone estimates standardization and learned parameters. Validation selects thresholds and method

Here µj and sj are ﬁtted on the ﬁtting set only; σ is the logistic function. We block when qi meets

All valid stored probabilities lie strictly between zero and one, so no clipping parameter is introduced.

H.1     Selection and uncertainty

For every entry, validation considers the native outcome and eligible numerical scores at cutoﬀs
0, 0.01, . . . , 1, plus an all-allow cutoﬀ above one. Categorical-only outputs have no cutoﬀ search.
The balanced objective maximizes balanced accuracy, then the smaller class recall, then macro-F1;
remaining ties prefer native decisions, cutoﬀs closest to 0.5, lower cutoﬀs, and lexical candidate
ID. Constrained objectives prioritize their stated recall/error requirement before these ties. An
unresolved compliant output consumes the false-block allowance. An infeasible entry is a gap, not
a silently relaxed constraint.
Common validation has 512 WildGuard and 1,920 HateXplain examples. Earlier ensemble ﬁtting
used 4,096 examples per task; the ﬁtted parameters are replayed, not re-estimated on this validation
set. Candidate eligibility requires aligned validation/test coverage, and earlier archive checks veri-
ﬁed ﬁtting-group separation. The available pool reﬂects earlier accuracy-based searches, including
candidates whose test outputs exist because they passed those earlier selections. It is neither an
equal-budget search nor an exhaustive policy inventory.
All test comparisons use 1,699 WildGuard requests (754 violations, 945 compliant) or 1,924 Ha-
teXplain posts (594 hate, 1,330 non-hate). The latter contains 1,922 duplicate groups. Intervals
use 2,000 paired duplicate-group bootstrap draws, seed 20261029. They are unadjusted descriptive
intervals, conditional on saved outputs. Pairwise diﬀerences use the same draws; subtracting two
marginal intervals is not the paired calculation. Search uncertainty, repeated-inference variability,
training exposure, and prior test inspection are not covered.


H.2     Metric deﬁnitions and unresolved outputs

Let P and N be the full numbers of reference violations and compliant cases, including unresolved
outputs. Let T P, T N, F P, F N count resolved binary outcomes, with U+ and U− unresolved on
violations and compliant cases. Then
                                   (        )
                                  TP    TN                TP + TN
                         BA = 21     +       , Accuracy =          ,
                                   P     N                 P +N                                   (2)
                          P = T P + F N + U+ ,       N = T N + F P + U− .

False-block rate is F P/N and missed-violation rate is F N/P ; the total compliant and violation
error burdens additionally include U− /N and U+ /P . Thus BA is one minus the average of the
two total class-error rates. Unresolved answers cannot become successes or disappear from recall
denominators.
Macro-F1 averages ALLOW and BLOCK F1; an unresolved answer contributes to the missed
count for its true class. MCC uses all four binary confusion counts and is omitted when unresolved
outputs occur. Precision is T P/(T P + F P ). AUROC and average precision are reported only for
available numerical scores, with score coverage stated in the CSV/JSON; they are not fabricated for
categorical outputs. They measure ordering, not calibration or a deployable cutoﬀ. Plain accuracy
and precision also depend on class prevalence.
Safeguard has nine unresolved WildGuard outcomes: seven harmful and two compliant. Its reported
58 false blocks and 109 misses exclude those nine, which remain errors in accuracy and class recalls.
Its coverage is 1,690/1,699; every other selected balanced entry has complete binary outputs. This
convention prevents apparent improvements from dropping hard failures.
